Finding an irregular heartbeat (AF) from a smartwatch pulse signal

A smartwatch shines a small light on your skin to read your pulse. We take 30 seconds of that pulse, describe it with 35 simple numbers, and a computer model decides: is the heartbeat irregular (AF) or normal? We tested it only on patients it had never seen before. It caught 98.99% of the irregular stretches.

The problem

Atrial Fibrillation (AF) is a heart rhythm problem. The top part of the heart fires wildly (350–600 times a minute), so the heartbeat has no pattern. AF makes a stroke 500% more likely and can lead to heart failure. It often comes and goes without the person feeling anything, so millions of people have it and do not know.

Our goal

Make a small, fast, accurate program that reads the pulse signal from a watch and labels every 30 seconds as AF or Normal. It answers in less than a thousandth of a second and is small enough to run on the tiny chip inside a smartwatch.

Try it: see the difference
Cleaned pulse signal One heartbeat (peak) Gap between two beats, in milliseconds
In everyday terms

Think of the heart as a drummer. A healthy drummer keeps a steady beat. In AF, the drummer has lost the rhythm completely. This project teaches a computer to hear that difference using only the light sensor on the back of a watch.

02 · The heart

What AF is, in simple words

The heart is a pump with two parts: a top part that fills up and a bottom part that pushes blood around the body. A tiny built-in timer tells both parts when to squeeze.

Normal heartbeat
  • The timer sends one clear signal each beat.
  • The top part squeezes, then the bottom part squeezes.
  • The beat is steady, like a clock: tick, tick, tick.
AF heartbeat
  • The timer loses control. The top part gets 350–600 messy signals a minute.
  • The top part just shivers instead of squeezing.
  • Beats come at random times: tick … ticktick … … tick.
In everyday terms

Imagine a band with one drummer keeping time. That is a normal heart. Now imagine ten people all banging drums at once, each at their own speed. Nobody can follow the beat. That is AF.

Why AF is dangerous
  1. Stroke. Because the top part only shivers, some blood sits still and can form a clot. If the clot travels to the brain, it causes a stroke. AF makes a stroke 500% more likely.
  2. A weaker pump. The heart pumps 20–30% less blood, so people feel tired, and over time it can lead to heart failure.
  3. Often no warning. AF can come and go without the person feeling anything. That is why a watch that checks all day is useful.

For the viva, the medical names are: the timer is the SA node, the top part is the atria, the bottom part is the ventricles, and the gate between them is the AV node.

03 · The sensor

Why use a watch light sensor (PPG) instead of an ECG

An ECG reads the heart's electrical signal with sticky pads on the chest. Doctors trust it most. But it is usually done for only 10 seconds in a clinic, or for 24–48 hours with a bulky Holter monitor. AF that comes and goes is easily missed.

PPG (photoplethysmography) is the light sensor in smartwatches, fitness bands and fingertip clips. A tiny green or infrared light shines into the skin and a sensor measures how much light comes back. Each heartbeat pushes more blood into the skin, and more blood soaks up more light. So the signal goes up and down with every beat. PPG is cheap, painless and can be worn all day and night.

In everyday terms

Hold your phone's torch against your fingertip. The finger glows red and flickers slightly with each heartbeat. That flicker is a PPG signal. A watch reads it all day without you noticing.

06 · Cleaning

Cleaning the signal and finding each heartbeat

The raw light signal is messy. Breathing makes it drift up and down slowly, and small movements add jitter. We clean it in four steps.

Raw signal→ A · Fill gaps→ B · Filter→ C · Same scale→ D · Find beats→ Beat gaps (IBI)
  1. A

    Fill the gaps

    Sometimes the sensor slips and a few readings are missing. We fill each gap with a straight line between the readings on either side (np.interp).

  2. B

    Keep only heartbeat speeds

    • Slow drifts below 0.5 Hz come from breathing and body movement, and they are removed.
    • Fast noise above 5 Hz comes from muscle activity and electronics, and it is removed.
    • The heart beats between about 30 and 300 beats per minute (0.5 to 5 Hz), so that is the range kept.
    • The filter is a 3rd-order Butterworth, applied with filtfilt in both directions, which is called zero-phase. This means peaks are not shifted in time, which matters because we measure the time between peaks.
    Why this Step B filter runs forwards and then backwards

    A normal filter makes the signal arrive a little late, which moves the peaks. The 0.5–5 Hz Butterworth filter in this step (scipy.signal.filtfilt) runs forwards and then backwards, so the two delays cancel out. Every beat stays at its true time. This matters because all 35 numbers depend on exact beat timing.

    In everyday terms: the filter is like noise-cancelling headphones that only let the heartbeat through.

  3. C

    Put everyone on the same scale

    Different sensors give bigger or smaller signals. We rescale each signal so its average is 0 and its spread is 1. This is called a Z‑score:

    z = x − μσ
    • x = one reading of the signal
    • μ (mu) = the average of the signal
    • σ (sigma) = the spread of the signal (standard deviation)

    In everyday terms: the Z-score is like converting marks from different schools to one scale so they can be compared.

  4. D

    Find the beats

    Each heartbeat makes a peak. We count a peak only if it is at least 0.27 s after the last one (264 ms, so at most about 222 beats a minute) and stands out clearly (at least 0.25 × the signal's spread).

    In everyday terms: a heart cannot beat twice that fast, so a second bump that close (the small “echo” after each beat) is ignored. Like a doorbell that cannot ring again for 0.27 s.

The gap between beats (IBI)

This is the most important measurement in the whole project. It needs two simple ideas: a peak and the gap between peaks.

Idea 1

What is a peak?

Every time your heart beats, the PPG signal goes up like a small hill. The top of that hill is a peak. One peak means one heartbeat.

Idea 2

What is the IBI?

The inter-beat interval (IBI) is the waiting time between one heartbeat and the next. Doctors call the same thing the R-R interval on an ECG.

Reading the numbers

IBI is measured in milliseconds (ms), where 1000 ms = 1 second. So 800 ms = 0.8 seconds between two beats. That equals about 75 beats per minute (60 ÷ 0.8), which is a normal heart rate.

What the example numbers mean

Normal rhythm
Beat 1 → 2800 ms = 0.800 ssteady
Beat 2 → 3810 ms = 0.810 ssteady
Beat 3 → 4795 ms = 0.795 ssteady
Beat 4 → 5805 ms = 0.805 ssteady
Beat 5 → 6800 ms = 0.800 ssteady

The gaps are almost identical, so the heart is ticking like a steady clock. This is a regular heartbeat.

AF (Atrial Fibrillation)
Beat 1 → 2620 ms = 0.620 squick
Beat 2 → 3910 ms = 0.910 sslow
Beat 3 → 4540 ms = 0.540 svery quick
Beat 4 → 5780 ms = 0.780 smedium
Beat 5 → 6500 ms = 0.500 svery quick

The gaps jump around randomly, so the heart is ticking like a broken clock. This is an irregular heartbeat, and it is the main sign of AF.

Why the IBI matters most

The whole project tries to answer one question: is the heartbeat steady or random? The IBI is what lets us answer it. Once we have the gaps between beats, we can compute numbers that describe how much they vary:

  • std_ibi tells us how spread out the gaps are.
  • rmssd and pnn50 tell us how much each gap differs from the one before it.

When those numbers are small, the rhythm is likely normal. When they are large, it is likely AF. Most of the 35 numbers the model uses (21 of them) are built from the IBI.

One-line version for your presentation

“IBI is the time gap between two heartbeats. In a healthy heart the gaps are almost equal; in AF they are random. Our model learns to spot that difference.”

Why 30 seconds, with half overlap

Two choices are made here: how long each clip is, and how much the clips overlap.

Choice 1

Why 30 seconds long?

  • Enough beats to judge. To tell “steady” from “random” we need many gaps. A 5-second clip has only about 6 beats, which is too few. A 30-second clip has 35–45 beats, which is enough.
  • Same as real watches. The Apple Watch and Fitbit also check your rhythm every 30 to 60 seconds.
Choice 2

Why start a new clip every 15 seconds?

  • Nothing gets cut in half. A short burst of AF that falls on the edge of one clip lands in the middle of the next one (see the example below).
  • More examples to learn from. One 20-minute recording gives 79 clips instead of 40, so 34 recordings become 2,686 clips.

Example: an AF burst from second 25 to second 35

Without overlap

Clips are 0–30 s and 30–60 s. The burst is split: 5 seconds in the first clip and 5 seconds in the second. Each clip sees only a small piece of AF, so it could be missed.

With overlap

There is also a clip from 15–45 s. The whole 10-second burst sits inside it, so the model sees the full AF event.

In everyday terms

It is like photographing a parade with overlapping shots. Without overlap, a float passing right at the switch would be half in each photo. With overlap, every float appears whole in at least one picture.

08 · Describing each clip

35 numbers that describe each clip

The model cannot look at a wavy line directly. So we describe each 30-second clip with 35 numbers, in 6 groups. Open a group to see its numbers.

In everyday terms

Instead of playing a whole song to someone, you give them a fact sheet: the tempo, how much the tempo wobbles, how loud each beat is, and how predictable the pattern is. These 35 numbers are that fact sheet for 30 seconds of pulse.

1. How much the beat gaps vary 12 numbers
mean_ibistd_ibi (SDNN)rmssdpnn50pnn20cv_ibimedian_ibiiqr_ibimin_ibimax_ibirange_ibihr_bpm

These measure how spread out the gaps between beats are (doctors call this pulse rate variability). rmssd measures how much each gap differs from the next. pnn50 and pnn20 are the share of gaps that change by more than 50 ms or 20 ms. iqr_ibi measures the spread of the middle half of the gaps, so one odd beat does not throw it off. It is the most useful number in the model.

Normal vs AF: iqr_ibi ≈ 15–25 ms vs ≈ 150–350 ms · rmssd 10 ms vs 267 ms · pnn50 0% vs >80%

2. Beat-gap scatter shape (Poincaré) 4 numbers
poincare_sd1sd2sd_ratioarea

We plot each gap against the next gap. SD1 is how wide the cloud of dots is (short-term changes; SD1 = RMSSD / √2). SD2 is how long it is (long-term changes). Normal makes a tight cluster; AF makes a big scattered cloud. See the Poincaré figure.

3. How unpredictable the rhythm is (entropy) 2 numbers
sample_entropy_ibishannon_entropy_ibi

These ask: if two beats follow a pattern, does the third beat follow it too? In a normal rhythm, patterns repeat, so the score is near 0. In AF, patterns never repeat, so the score is high. (Settings: m = 2, r = 0.2 × spread.) Among the top 4 most useful numbers.

4. How the beat strength changes 4 numbers
mean_peak_ampstd_peak_ampcv_peak_amppeak_count

In AF the heart has a different amount of time to fill before each beat, so some beats are strong and some are weak (the Frank-Starling law). These numbers measure that. peak_count is the number of beats in the clip. cv_peak_amp ranks #7 of the 35 numbers.

5. Shape of the wave 5 numbers
ppg_meanppg_stdppg_skewppg_kurtosiszero_crossing_rate

Simple statistics of the wave: its average, its spread, whether it rises faster than it falls (skew), how sharp the peaks are (kurtosis), and how often it crosses the middle line.

6. Rhythm “frequencies” (Welch spectrum) 8 numbers
vlf_powerlf_powerhf_powercardiac_powerlf_hf_ratiototal_spectral_powerspectral_entropydominant_freq

Like a music equaliser, this shows which speeds of change are strongest in the signal. Welch's method splits the clip into overlapping 4-second pieces and averages them, which gives a smooth, reliable result. A steady pulse has one strong speed (the heart rate). AF spreads its energy everywhere, so spectral_entropy is high. It is the #3 most useful number.

Safety rule

If a clip is so noisy that fewer than 3 beats are found, the 21 numbers based on beat gaps are set to 0.0. This stops the program from crashing. The model handles these zeros fine.

10 · Fair testing

Fair testing: never test on a patient the model has seen

The common mistake

If we shuffled all the clips and split them at random, clips from the same person would end up in both the training set and the test set (neighbouring clips even share 15 seconds). The model would learn to recognise each person's unique pulse shape instead of learning AF. It would score about 99.8% in testing and then fail on new patients. This mistake is called data leakage.

In everyday terms

It is like a teacher giving the final exam questions out as practice. Everyone scores 99%, but nobody has learned anything. Splitting by patient means the “exam” always uses people the model has never met.

What we did instead

  1. Locked away 9 test patients. 9 patients (5 AF, 4 normal; 711 clips) were set aside at the very start (random seed 42, so anyone can repeat it). The model never saw them while it was being built.
  2. Practised on the other 25 patients in 5 rounds. The 25 patients (1,975 clips) were split into 5 groups. Each round, the model trained on 4 groups and was checked on the 5th. This is called 5-fold cross-validation (StratifiedGroupKFold).
    • All 79 clips of one patient always stay in the same group.
    • Each group gets a fair mix of AF and normal patients.
  3. Scored two ways.
    • Per clip: was each 30-second clip labelled correctly?
    • Per patient: average the AF chance over all 79 clips. If the average is 0.5 or more, the patient is labelled AF.
11 · The models

Three models we compared

A model is the part that looks at the 35 numbers of a clip and decides: AF or Normal. We tried three models. All three are built from the same building block, the decision tree. They differ in how many trees they use and how those trees work together. All three were tested on exactly the same patient groups, so the comparison is fair.

One tree→Many trees that vote→Many trees that learn from each other
Model 1 · the simple baseline

Decision Tree

A single flowchart of yes/no questions.

Everyday pictureOne doctor with a checklist.

How it works, step by step

  1. It looks at all the practice clips and finds the one question that best separates AF from Normal, for example “Is iqr_ibi above 60 ms?”.
  2. It splits the clips into two groups by that answer.
  3. Inside each group it finds the next best question, and keeps going.
  4. It stops after 6 levels of questions. Each end point (a “leaf”) gives the answer: AF or Normal.

Example: one 30-second clip

A clip has very uneven beat gaps. The tree asks “Is iqr_ibi above 60 ms?” → Yes. Then “Is pnn50 high?” → Yes. It reaches the leaf AF. One path, one answer.

Made-up example to show the idea.

Model 2 · many trees that vote

Random Forest

100 different trees that each give an answer, then vote.

Everyday picture100 doctors who each look separately, then vote.

How it works, step by step

  1. It builds 100 decision trees.
  2. Each tree is trained on a random part of the practice clips and numbers, so every tree turns out a little different.
  3. For a new clip, all 100 trees give their answer separately.
  4. The answer with the most votes wins.

Example: one 30-second clip

For one clip, 82 of the 100 trees vote AF and 18 vote Normal. The forest answers AF, with an AF chance of about 82%.

Model 3 · our choice

LightGBM

Small trees built one after another, each fixing the mistakes of the ones before. This is called “gradient boosting”.

Everyday picture150 doctors in a line. Each one studies the cases the previous doctors got wrong and fixes them.

How it works, step by step

  1. Tree 1 makes a rough first guess for every practice clip.
  2. The program checks where that guess was wrong.
  3. Tree 2 is trained only to fix those mistakes. Then tree 3 fixes what is still wrong, and so on, 150 times.
  4. For a new clip, the small answers of all 150 trees are added up into one AF chance. 0.5 or more means AF.

Example: one 30-second clip

Tree 1 says “maybe AF”. Trees 2, 3, 4 … each add a small correction, mostly pushing towards AF. After 150 trees the total is an AF chance of 0.93, which is above 0.5, so the answer is AF.

Made-up example to show the idea.

Good at

  • Fastest: answers in under a millisecond.
  • Tiny: less than 200 KB of memory, so it fits on a watch.
  • Caught 98.99% of AF in the 9 new test patients.

Weak at

  • Has more settings to tune.
  • In the practice rounds it scored 0.81% below Random Forest (a gap well inside the normal ±12% variation).

Settings used in this project

  • n_estimators=150 150 trees.
  • learning_rate=0.05 each tree makes only a small correction.
  • num_leaves=31 at most 31 answers per tree.
  • max_depth=6 at most 6 questions in a row.
  • subsample=0.8 each tree sees 80% of the clips.
  • colsample_bytree=0.8 each tree sees 80% of the 35 numbers.

So why LightGBM?

Random Forest scored 0.81% higher in practice, but that gap is well inside the ±12% round-to-round variation, so the two are about equally accurate. LightGBM is much smaller and faster, which is what a watch needs, and it caught 98.99% of AF in the 9 new test patients.

Why LightGBM is fast enough for a watch
  1. Histogram binning: it sorts values into 256 buckets. Instead of comparing every exact number, it only checks 256 buckets. That is much less work.
  2. Leaf-wise growth: it grows the most useful branch first. It always splits the leaf that reduces the error most, so it needs fewer questions to get the answer right.

In everyday terms: why false alarms are OK here

The model almost never misses AF (4 out of 395), but it gives a false alarm for about 1 in 4 normal clips (79 of 316). For a watch that screens people, that is the right trade. Why patient non_af_014 was wrong

This patient had a normal rhythm, but the model said AF (0.9301). Two reasons:

  1. Frequent early beats. The patient often had an extra early beat followed by a long pause (doctors call these PACs, or ectopic beats). In 30 seconds, 3 or 4 of these pauses make the gaps look very uneven, so iqr_ibi and pnn50 go up, just like in AF.
  2. A very weak pulse signal. Poor blood flow made the signal tiny, so the beat times were slightly shaky.

How to fix it: look at 2 minutes instead of 30 seconds, or add a filter that ignores early beats.

In everyday terms

An early beat is like a drummer who hits one beat too soon and then pauses to catch up. In a short 30-second clip, that hiccup can sound like a drummer who has lost the rhythm. Listening for longer makes the difference clear.

2. Which numbers mattered most

LightGBM counts how often it used each number to make a decision (its split count). The top ones are exactly the signs doctors look for.

  • iqr_ibi (347) and pnn50 (262): uneven gaps between beats, the main sign of AF.
  • spectral_entropy and sample_entropy_ibi: how random and patternless the rhythm is.
  • cv_peak_amp (#7): how much beat strength changes, which adds information beyond timing.

Because the model relies on the same signs doctors use, its decisions can be explained. It is not a black box. Note: a split count shows how often a number was used, not whether high or low values mean AF (see Question 7).

8 hard questions examiners may ask

These are the weak spots. Bring them up yourself and answer calmly.

Question 1 · Choice of model
“Random Forest scored a little higher in practice (88.15% vs 87.34%). Why choose LightGBM?”
Do not say LightGBM beat Random Forest. The gap is only 0.81%, and all three models are within the ±12% round-to-round variation.
Say: “Random Forest is slightly higher in practice (+0.81%). But LightGBM trains much faster, answers in under a millisecond and uses less than 200 KB of memory, so it fits on a smartwatch. It also caught 98.99% of AF in the new patients.”
Question 2 · Big ± values
“Why do the practice scores vary so much (±11.9% accuracy, ±12.3% AF caught)?”
Big variation can look like an unstable model if you do not explain it.
Say: “With 25 patients in 5 rounds, each round checks only about 5 patients. People's bodies differ a lot, so one unusual patient can move a round's score by about 20%. The variation comes from the small number of patients, not from the model.”
Question 3 · Only 9 test patients
“Are 9 test patients enough to call this hospital-ready?”
Never claim it is ready for hospitals based on 9 people.
Say: “No. 88.89% is simply 8 out of 9 patients. One more mistake would change it by 11.1%. This is a first working version and a public benchmark. It must be tested on hundreds of patients before any medical approval.”
Question 4 · False alarms
“75% specificity means 1 in 4 normal clips gives a false alarm. Isn't that bad?”
In the general public only 1–2% of people have AF, so false alarms can add up.
Say: “Yes, and it is on purpose. A missed AF can lead to a stroke that could have been prevented. A false alarm only leads to a harmless ECG to check. For screening, catching 98.99% of AF comes first.”
Question 5 · Tuning the settings
“Why didn't you search for the best settings (GridSearchCV or Optuna)?”
Examiners check whether settings were quietly tuned to look good.
Say: “We used fixed, standard settings (150 trees, lr=0.05, num_leaves=31) for every model. That keeps the comparison fair and stops test information leaking into the settings. Searching with Optuna is planned for future work.”
Question 6 · Hospital vs everyday life
“Will a model trained on hospital patients work on an Apple Watch while people walk around?”
Hospital patients lie still; watch users walk, run and wave their arms.
Say: “Not on its own. Hospital patients lie still, but wrist movement adds a lot of noise. A real watch would also need its motion sensor (a 3-axis accelerometer) and a filter (adaptive RLS) to remove that noise.”
Question 7 · Feature importance
“iqr_ibi has the highest split count. Does that mean higher iqr_ibi means AF?”
A split count shows how often a number was used, not which direction points to AF.
Say: “No. It only counts how many times iqr_ibi was used as a question across the 150 trees. We know the direction from medical knowledge and from working examples by hand.”
Question 8 · Using the right medical word
“Does AF really cause ‘pulsus alternans’?”
Pulsus alternans strictly means strong-weak-strong-weak beats in a regular rhythm, seen in severe heart failure.
Say: “Strictly, no. Pulsus alternans means beat strength alternates in a regular rhythm. In AF the rhythm is random, so the correct term is beat-to-beat variation in pulse strength, caused by different filling times (the Frank-Starling law).”

17 · Being honest

Limits and next steps

Four limits to mention yourself
  1. Only 34 patients. 2,686 clips is a lot of data, but it comes from few people (19 AF, 15 normal). One unusual patient dropped patient accuracy from 100% to 88.9%. Real approval needs huge studies like the Apple Heart Study or UK Biobank.
  2. Patients were lying still. In daily life, walking and arm movement create noise at the same speeds as the heartbeat (0.5–5.0 Hz).
  3. Only two answers. The model says AF or Normal. It cannot yet spot other problems such as Atrial Flutter, early beats (PACs and PVCs), or very slow or fast heart rates.
  4. False alarms. With 75.0% specificity, a watch worn by healthy people (where only 1–2% have AF) would give some false alarms. A second check, or looking at longer clips (e.g. 2 minutes), would help.

What we would do next

1

Remove movement noise

Combine the light sensor with the watch's motion sensor (3-axis accelerometer), and use a filter (adaptive RLS or Kalman) to subtract arm movement.

2

Recognise more heart problems

Teach the model to spot early beats (PACs/PVCs) separately, which would fix false alarms like patient non_af_014, and to flag Atrial Flutter.

3

Run it on a tiny chip

Turn the trained trees into plain C code (with m2cgen or ONNX-Runtime) so it runs on under 10 mW on a smart ring or watch chip (ARM Cortex-M4 / BLE).

1. Clean the signal and rescale it

import numpy as np
from scipy import signal

def bandpass_filter(sig: np.ndarray, fs: float = 125.0) -> np.ndarray:
    """Zero-phase 3rd-order Butterworth bandpass filter (0.5 to 5.0 Hz)."""
    # 1. Fill missing points via linear interpolation
    if np.isnan(sig).any():
        nans = np.isnan(sig)
        x = np.arange(len(sig))
        sig = np.interp(x, x[~nans], sig[~nans])

    # 2. Design Butterworth filter
    nyq = 0.5 * fs
    b, a = signal.butter(3, [0.5 / nyq, 5.0 / nyq], btype='band')

    # 3. Apply forwards and backwards for zero phase shift
    return signal.filtfilt(b, a, sig)

def zscore(sig: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Standardizes signal to mean 0 and standard deviation 1."""
    return (sig - np.mean(sig)) / (np.std(sig) + 1e-6)

2. Find the beats and the gaps between them

def detect_peaks_and_ibis(window: np.ndarray, fs: float = 125.0):
    """Detects systolic peaks with 0.27s refractory rule and adaptive prominence."""
    min_dist = int(0.27 * fs)  # 33 samples = 264 ms refractory period
    prominence = 0.25 * (np.std(window) if np.std(window) > 1e-6 else 1.0)

    peaks, _ = signal.find_peaks(window, distance=min_dist, prominence=prominence)

    if len(peaks) >= 3:
        ibis = np.diff(peaks) / fs * 1000.0  # gaps in milliseconds
        return peaks, ibis

    # Safety fallback: returns empty array if fewer than 3 peaks
    return peaks, np.array([])

AF (Atrial Fibrillation)A heart rhythm problem: the top chambers shiver (350–600 times a minute), the beat has no pattern, and stroke risk goes up 5 times.
PPGA pulse signal read with light through the skin, as in smartwatches and finger clips. It rises and falls with each heartbeat.
IBI (gap between beats)The time between two heartbeats in milliseconds. Steady in normal rhythm (~800 ms), jumpy in AF (620, 910, 540, 780 ms). Same idea as the R-R interval on an ECG.
PRVPulse rate variability: how much the gaps between beats change. The light-sensor version of heart rate variability (HRV).
RMSSDHow much each gap differs from the next one, on average. Much higher in AF (267 ms vs 10 ms).
pNN50 / pNN20The share of neighbouring gaps that differ by more than 50 ms or 20 ms. About 0% in normal rhythm, over 80% in AF.
Poincaré plotA dot plot of each gap against the next gap. A tight bunch for normal, a scattered cloud for AF.
Sample entropyA score for how random the rhythm is. Low when patterns repeat, high in AF (settings m = 2, r = 0.2σ).
Beat strength variationBeats that are sometimes strong and sometimes weak, because the heart fills for different times in AF (Frank-Starling law). Sometimes wrongly called pulsus alternans.
Zero-phase filterA filter run forwards and then backwards (filtfilt) so beats stay at their true times.
Refractory period (0.27 s)The short rest after a beat when the heart cannot beat again. Caps the count at 222 beats a minute and stops the small “echo” bump being counted as a beat.
Data leakageWhen test data sneaks into training, giving fake high scores. We avoid it by keeping each patient's clips together.
Stratified Group K-FoldSplitting patients into practice groups so each patient stays in one group and each group has a fair mix of AF and normal.
LightGBMA fast, small model made of many decision trees, built one after another, each fixing the last one's mistakes.
